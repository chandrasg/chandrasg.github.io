READING GUIDE — DEPRESSION, LANGUAGE & RACE

Paper: Rai et al. (2024), Key language markers of depression on social media depend on race. PNAS 121(14), e2319837121.
https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2319837121
Original public study data: https://osf.io/hkep7/

1. MODEL RESULTS (TABLE 1)
Change the training group and language representation to compare the reported correlations with depression severity in White and Black test groups. All eight correlations and associated p values are transcribed from Table 1. The chart uses a common zero-to-one scale. Pearson r is a correlation, not diagnostic accuracy. The paper prints some p values as .000; these are rounded values. The p values concern each correlation, not a direct comparison between groups.

2. LANGUAGE MARKERS (FIGURES 1 AND 2)
The explorer shows original public group means for I-usage and the five topics highlighted in Figure 2: outsider/belongingness, self-criticism, worthlessness/self-deprecation, anxious outsider, and despair. These come from the LIWC_languageMarkers.csv and Fb_LanguageMarkers.csv releases, with topic labels/keywords from Fb_TopicMapping.csv. Each point is a group average for a PHQ-9 score and racial group, in the original data units. This view lets readers inspect those averages; the paper's figures show fitted relationships. No trend lines or model results have been estimated from the averages. The slider highlights a questionnaire score. A dash indicates no group average at that score.

3. STUDY CONTEXT
The study included 868 English-speaking US adults, with 434 Black and 434 White participants matched on age and gender; 76% were women. Participants completed PHQ-9 and consented to share Facebook status updates. Findings concern this sample, platform, and measured features. They do not establish the cause of the differences and do not imply the absence of linguistic expressions of depression in any group. The webpage offers no individual assessment.
