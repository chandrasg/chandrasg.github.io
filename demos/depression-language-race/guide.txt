DEPRESSION, LANGUAGE AND RACE: PUBLIC-DATA COMPANION

Rai, Sunny; Stade, Elizabeth C.; Giorgi, Salvatore; Francisco, Ashley; Ungar, Lyle H.; Curtis, Brenda; Guntuku, Sharath C. (2024). Key language markers of depression on social media depend on race. PNAS 121(14), e2319837121.
Paper: https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2319837121
Public source release: https://osf.io/hkep7/

PURPOSE AND SCOPE

This is a partial reproduction of public outputs. It exposes all 1,404 released language-marker means, recovers 20 counts from an original public histogram, and supplies explicitly aggregate descriptive calculations. The original participant-level regressions, matching, depression-prediction models and cross-validation are not reproduced. This page gives no predictions, scores, diagnoses or recommendations for individual people.

The page contains 54 PHQ-score/race cells: 27 per group, each with three LIWC and 23 LDA means. White PHQ 25 and Black PHQ 26 are missing from the release and remain absent. All 23 source topic mappings are represented; repeated themes retain distinct topic IDs. No synthetic participants or language samples are introduced.

INTERPRETING THE EXPLORER

Each scatter point is the mean for one PHQ-score/race group. Cell sample sizes, within-cell variances and age/gender covariates are unavailable. Optional lines give all 27 observed cells equal weight; they cannot estimate participant-weighted or covariate-adjusted coefficients, uncertainty or individual-level effects. The chart preserves original CSV units. Raw LDA means are not the normalized topic scores shown in the paper’s Figure 2.

The paper’s observations concern its studied sample and measured language features. The explorer does not establish that associations generalize to every person, community or context.

HISTOGRAM AND NORMALITY DIAGNOSTIC

Ten histogram bins per group are recovered from the original vector graphic. Counts sum to 434 Black and 432 White participants, versus the paper’s 434 per group. The cause of the two-count White shortfall is unknown. Printed x-axis labels are preserved; exact bin edges and individual values are not inferred. The redraw recovers the numerical output of a released graphic, rather than recomputing a histogram from participant records.

A Shapiro–Wilk test on the 27 released Black I-usage means gives W=0.9810224231 and p=0.8846822803. These round to .98/.88, the pair in the final supplement, but cannot identify what the authors tested or verify a normality test over 434 individuals. A nonsignificant normality-test p value does not establish equality of distributions or variances.

REPORTED TABLES AND SOURCE VERSIONS

The eight Pearson correlations and eight printed p values in Table 1 are transcribed source results. All 16 displays agree between the final paper and OSF manuscript. They are not newly obtained predictions.

The final PNAS article and published supplement each have three pages. A separate nine-page OSF author manuscript additionally contains Tables S1/S2 and extra figures. Its 260 table cells are retained as source transcriptions with version attribution. The detailed tables are not in the final published supplement.

In source Table S2, the Anger row repeats Worry keywords and a Despair row repeats Emptiness-longing keywords. Those two rows retain null topic IDs because their correspondence is unresolved. Other correspondences use theme names and distinctive overlapping keywords; the source table prints no topic IDs. This does not alter the complete 23-topic CSV explorer.

Applying Benjamini–Hochberg separately to each table’s printed interaction p values gives 1/3 and 5/23 rejections at .05. This is arithmetic on rounded source summaries. The original full-precision p values and correction-family code are unavailable; the calculation is not a regression refit.
